The missing piece is getting your Wavelength EC2 instance to push commands down to the Pi's SIM over the Verizon carrier network. Here's how that works:

The Core Networking Concept
The critical rule (mentioned earlier) is:

Inbound to Wavelength from internet = blocked
Outbound from the Pi (carrier network) to Wavelength = allowed

This means you cannot have Wavelength initiate the connection to the Pi. The Pi must initiate and maintain a persistent connection up to Wavelength.

How to Bridge the Gap
The Pattern: Persistent Outbound Connection from Pi
[Your UI]
    ↓ (internet)
[Wavelength EC2]
    ↑ (persistent connection, carrier network only)
[Raspberry Pi + SIM]
    ↓ (serial/GPIO)
[Radio Transmitter]
    ↓ (RF)
[Drone]
The Pi opens and holds a connection to Wavelength — your relay registration system is exactly the right idea for this.

Best Protocol Options for the Persistent Connection
Option A — MQTT (recommended for drone telemetry)

Pi runs an MQTT client, connects outbound to a broker on your Wavelength instance
Lightweight, designed for unreliable networks
Bi-directional once connected — Wavelength can push commands down
AWS IoT Core supports MQTT natively if you want to extend later

Option B — WebSocket

Pi opens a WebSocket to Wavelength
Good if your UI is already web-based
Easy to integrate with existing relay registration logic

Option C — Reverse SSH Tunnel

Pi SSHs into Wavelength and opens a reverse tunnel
Simple but not ideal for production
Good for testing your relay system right now


Fleshing Out Your Relay Registration
Your placeholder relay registration should do these things:
1. Pi boots → connects SIM → gets carrier IP
2. Pi sends registration packet to Wavelength:
   {device_id, auth_token, carrier_ip, timestamp}
3. Wavelength logs the connection, keeps socket open
4. UI sends command → Wavelength → down the open socket → Pi → radio TX
5. Pi sends telemetry back up the same connection

This is the most important thing to understand. There are two separate control layers:

You don't manually fly it in real time — you send it mission commands (loiter at X,Y, altitude Z) and ArduPlane handles the actual flying.
Layer 2 — Mission Commands (CM4 + Wavelength handles this)
Your CM4 talks to the Pixhawk over the internal TELEM2/serial connection using MAVLink protocol. Your Wavelength EC2 sends MAVLink commands down to the CM4, which relays them to the Pixhawk:
Your UI

  ↓ (Verizon 5G, MQTT/WebSocket)

  ↓ (MAVLink over internal TELEM2 serial)
Pixhawk (ArduPlane)
  ↓ (PWM signals)

Why Starlink, Not LTE
1. LTE Infrastructure Gets Destroyed
In active conflict zones, cell towers are priority targets. They're fixed, known locations, easy to strike. In many parts of eastern Ukraine, LTE coverage is either destroyed, degraded, or completely absent. Starlink works where there are no towers left standing.
2. LTE Coverage is Sparse Outside Cities
Ukraine's LTE network is strong in cities but very patchy in rural/frontline areas — exactly where drones operate. A drone flying over fields east of Zaporizhzhia or in Kherson oblast has little to no LTE coverage.
3. Starlink is Low Earth Orbit
Starlink satellites are at ~550km altitude vs traditional satellite at 35,000km. Latency is 20-40ms — comparable to what we were estimating for LTE-to-Frankfurt. It's genuinely competitive.
4. Starlink is Mobile and Deployable
A unit can set up a Starlink terminal anywhere in 5 minutes. No infrastructure dependency whatsoever.
